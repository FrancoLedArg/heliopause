# Gravity assists: how they work, real figures, and a simple game model

Researched 2026-11-14 for ticket #74 (post-v1 planning, design backlog #11 "Gravity assists"). This file changes no v1 code or spec. Sections 1 to 3 are sourced facts; section 4 is a design suggestion, kept separate on purpose. Anything not confirmed from a primary page is marked UNVERIFIED.

## Short answers

1. A flyby changes the spacecraft's direction relative to the planet but not its speed relative to the planet; because the planet is itself moving round the Sun, the turned path leaves the spacecraft moving faster (or slower) relative to the Sun. The planet pays for it with an immeasurably small change in its own motion.
2. Real assists range from about 2.7 km/s (Parker, Venus, slowing down) through about 3.9 km/s (New Horizons, Jupiter) to about 16 km/s (each Voyager, Jupiter; the 16 km/s figure is UNVERIFIED, derived from an unopened search excerpt). Trip-time savings are quoted for Voyager 2 (Neptune in 12 years instead of 30) and New Horizons (3 years).
3. Rule of thumb: the Sun-relative speed change is at most about twice the planet's orbital speed, reached only by a very close, very slow approach. This bound is standard orbital mechanics, NOT stated on any NASA page I could open (UNVERIFIED as a NASA statement). The real figures above sit well under it.
4. Suggested game rule: a flyby of a Body on the way to a farther target credits a share of that Body's orbital speed against the Transfer Burn only, with a larger share for Gas Giants. See section 4.

## 1. How a gravity assist works

Plain paragraph for beginners: A planet is a huge object moving round the Sun at high speed. When a spacecraft swings past it, the planet's gravity pulls the spacecraft in and then lets it go again, so that, from the planet's point of view, it leaves just as fast as it arrived but in a new direction. But the planet is itself travelling, so when you add the planet's own motion back in, a spacecraft that leaves heading the same way the planet is going is now moving much faster relative to the Sun than before; one that leaves the other way is slower. The spacecraft did not burn anything: it borrowed a tiny sliver of the planet's enormous orbital motion. A heavier planet has more gravity to bend the path sharply, and a faster-moving planet has more motion to lend, so both give bigger assists.

Sources and what each backs:

- NASA, Basics of Space Flight, Chapter 4 "Trajectories", https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter4-1/ . Says "angular momentum is transferred from the orbiting planet to a spacecraft"; the spacecraft leaves "carrying additional orbital angular momentum stolen from Jupiter"; Jupiter's loss "is too small to be measured"; a spacecraft "may double its speed as the result of a gravity assist" yet "feels no acceleration at all". Without Jupiter, Voyager 2's orbit would have had "Perihelion ... at 1 au, and aphelion at Jupiter's distance of about 5 au". Flying in front of the planet's path decelerates: "Galileo lost energy in relation to Jupiter, helping it achieve Jupiter orbit insertion". (Quotes are from the fetched page, which I read through a summarising fetch tool, so treat wording as close, not letter-exact.)
- NASA Basics of Space Flight, "A Gravity Assist Primer", https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/primer/ . Speeds in the figures are "with respect to Jupiter"; the Sun-relative boost comes from Jupiter's orbital motion; "trajectory bending is the other key"; Voyager launch vehicles "could provide only enough energy to get them to Jupiter"; launch time "was planned so that Jupiter coasted by at just the right time"; the train analogy (ball at 30 MPH, train at 50 MPH, rebound near 130 MPH relative to the ground).
- NASA Goddard glossary, https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/vis/a010000/a014100/a014142/script_23617_18.html : "The orbit of a planet around the Sun is used as a source of energy"; moving the same way as the planet gains energy, and an assist can also decrease energy.

Why heavier and faster planets give more (reasoning, standard physics, UNVERIFIED as a NASA statement): a heavier planet bends a given approach more sharply at the same closest distance, and the planet's orbital speed sets how much can be added in the Sun's frame. ## 3. Limits and costs

- **Timing and alignment.** Voyager: "Their launch time was planned so that Jupiter coasted by at just the right time" (Primer). The outer-planet layout used by the Grand Tour "occurs about every 175 years" (Planetary Voyage page). New Horizons: see the UNVERIFIED five to six year delay above.
- **Closer is more, but not too close.** The Primer says the spacecraft passes close "but not close enough to impact". No NASA page I opened gives a numeric periapsis rule. Galileo's flybys show how close planners go: 188 miles above Earth.
- **Trade-offs.** Assists can lengthen trips (Galileo VEEGA: two to six years) and cost launch-window flexibility. They can also slow a spacecraft (Parker, Galileo at Io).
- **Rule of thumb for maximum Δv. UNVERIFIED as a NASA statement.** In the standard patched-conic analysis, the speed relative to the planet is unchanged, only its direction turns by an angle set by the planet's mass, the approach speed and the closest distance. The largest possible change in Sun-relative speed is therefore about twice the planet's orbital speed (a full 180 degree turn, impossible in practice), and the real change is the planet-relative speed times 2 sin(half the turn angle) at most, in the direction of the turn. A 1965 NASA technical report (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19650024859, scan, text not readable by my tools) is reported by search to derive maximum gains of this kind, including a Jupiter figure of about 42.5 km/s: **UNVERIFIED**, I could not read it. The only NASA-page statement I confirmed is qualitative: a spacecraft "may double its speed". Use as a guide only: the maximum is capped by planet orbital speed, and heavier planets let you get closer to the cap at a safe distance.

## 4. A simplified game model (design suggestion, not sourced fact)

This section is mine. It is built to sit on top of the v1 Hop model without changing it (`src/game/sim/hop.ts`): a Hop has three Burns, Takeoff and Landing each cost 0.835 times the Body's escape velocity (Mars's ascent Δv over escape velocity), and the Transfer is Hohmann-like: a boost to an ellipse round the star, `excessSpeed = departureBurn(starMu, fromDistance, toDistance)`, plus the climb out of the departure planet's well, `sqrt(excess^2 + 2mu/r) - sqrt(mu/r)`. The Δv an Engine then needs comes from the rocket equation in `spacecraft.ts`. Gravity assists would only reduce the star-frame excess speed.

**Rule A: flyby credit.** If a Hop's target orbits farther from the star than the departure Body and another planet sits between them (by distance from the star), the player may route past it. The Transfer's `excessSpeed` is reduced by `k x (that planet's orbital speed)`, never below zero, and Takeoff and Landing are not changed. The credit lowers `excessSpeed` inside `transferBurn`, so the climb term `sqrt(excessSpeed^2 + 2mu/r) - sqrt(mu/r)` shrinks with it; only the well depth (`2mu/r`) is untouched. `k` = 0.3 for a Gas Giant and 0.08 for a rocky planet. Those values come from the ratios in section 2: 0.30 (New Horizons at Jupiter) and 0.08 to 0.09 (Parker at Venus). The Voyager Jupiter ratio of 1.2 (UNVERIFIED) would be a best case that the game ignores. Each `k` rests on a single mission datum (0.3 on New Horizons, 0.08 on Parker), and the rocky `k` comes from a slowing flyby (Parker at Venus), not a speed-up. Optionally, a mass-scaled `k` between the two classes (Saturn, Uranus, Neptune lighter than Jupiter) can follow the same table.

**Rule B: slingshot to slow.** A Hop to a target nearer the star than the departure Body can use the same credit against its excess speed, for the inward case (Parker, Venus). Same `k`, same limits. This keeps the rule symmetric and avoids a second rule.

What each simplification gets wrong:

- Credit is a fixed share of orbital speed. Real gain depends on the approach speed, the closest distance, and which side of the planet you pass. The 1.2 versus 0.30 versus 0.08 spread shows how much a single `k` hides.
- Orbital speed is used as the scale, which is the right cap (twice the planet's speed) but the wrong detail: the real gain also grows with the planet's mass and shrinks with a fast approach, so a Gas Giant really gives more for two reasons, which `k` lumps into one number.
- The route ignores alignment. Real assists need the planet to be in the right place (launch windows, the 175-year Grand Tour). If the game has no clock, it can either ignore this (always aligned) or add one fixed, visible "window open or closed" flag per Hop. The first is wrong about Voyager-style opportunities, the second is only a nod to it.
- No cost or risk of close passes (no atmosphere, no radiation, no navigation error). v1's dispersion rule could optionally apply to the credit, so it is a probability rather than a promise.
- It never lengthens the trip. Real assists often add time (Galileo's VEEGA took two to six years; Cassini's 6.7 years is not compared with an unassisted time, so it is UNVERIFIED as an example). If the game tracks time, it should add some.
- Multiple planets in a row (Cassini VVEJGA) would just add credits in this model, which overstates stacking. Cap total credit at, say, the unreduced `excessSpeed`.
- Moons: no credit for a Moon flyby (too small, ignoring Galileo's Io saving). Fine for a first version.

## 5. Plain sentences for the game to show

- "A heavier, faster planet has more to lend, so a Gas Giant flyby saves more fuel than a small rocky one."
