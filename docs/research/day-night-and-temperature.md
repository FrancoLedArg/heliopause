## 1. The Moon

### Length of day and night

- **Synodic period (Sun to Sun, i.e. one full day-night cycle): 29.53 days.** NASA NSSDCA Moon Fact Sheet, https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html. Same sheet: sidereal rotation period 655.720 hours.
- Day and night each take about half a cycle, so roughly 14.8 Earth days each (our arithmetic, 29.53 / 2; at the equator).
- ESA/DLR: surface elements "have to cope with a Lunar night of about 2 weeks"; "the longest possible night is 16 days or 384 h" (polar landing sites excepted). Ulamec, Biele, Trollope, "How to survive a Lunar night", Planetary and Space Science 58 (2010) 1985-1995, DOI 10.1016/j.pss.2010.09.024. Text read from the PDF at https://ideas.esa.int/core/apps/IMT/UploadedFiles/00/f_89b0938046d43f992ac462e892108ca9/How_to_survive_a_Lunar_night.pdf
- JAXA: "The lunar surface alternates between 14 days of daylight and 14 days of night." SLIM project review briefing, https://www.isas.jaxa.jp/en/topics/files/SLIM-press-briefing-20241226.pdf
- NASA's Moon facts page gives the orbit as "27 Earth days" and says the Moon spins at that same rate, https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/. That is the sidereal period; the Sun-relative day is the longer 29.53 days above.

### The "dark side of the Moon" myth

The Moon always shows the same face to Earth, because it spins once for every orbit. The far side, the half we never see from Earth, is not dark: it gets just as much sunlight as the near side, and at New Moon, when the near side is dark, the far side is fully lit. NASA says calling it the "dark side" is "misleading" (https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/). Every part of the Moon has about two weeks of day and two weeks of night; "far" means never seen from Earth, not never lit.

### The commonly quoted -270 °C

-270 °C is not a place on the Moon. It is about 2.7 K, the temperature of deep space (the cosmic background radiation). Ulamec et al. 2010 use "deep space Tsky = 2.7 K" as the radiative sink for a lander. 2.7 K is -270.45 °C (our arithmetic). The attribution to the cosmic microwave background is general knowledge; no NASA CMB page was fetched, so that attribution is UNVERIFIED here. The coldest measured Moon surface is about 25 K (-248 °C), well above it.

## 2. Other Bodies

### How distance from the star sets temperature (plain terms)

A planet warms until the sunlight it absorbs equals the heat it radiates back to space. That balance temperature is the planetary equilibrium (NASA's fact sheets call it "black-body temperature"). Sunlight weakens with the square of distance (solar irradiance = 1361 W/m² at Earth, NSSDCA, https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html), and radiated heat grows with the fourth power of temperature, so the equilibrium temperature falls with the **square root** of distance: double the distance, about 0.71 of the temperature in kelvin. This scaling and the arithmetic below are ours (derived); the fact sheet itself gave no formula on the notes page we opened.

NSSDCA black-body temperatures: Earth 254.0 K (Bond albedo 0.294, irradiance 1361.0 W/m²); Mars 209.8 K (Bond albedo 0.250, irradiance 586.2 W/m²), https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html ; Mercury 439.6 K, https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/mercuryfact.html ; Moon 270.4 K (Moon fact sheet). Check of the scaling from Earth's 254 K, same albedo (derived): Mars at 1.52 AU gives about 206 K (sheet 209.8 K); Mercury at 0.39 AU about 408 K (sheet 439.6 K; albedo differs); a body at Jupiter's 5.2 AU about 111 K, close to Europa's equatorial mean below. Equilibrium temperature ignores atmosphere (greenhouse effect) and day/night swing: it is an average, not a noon or midnight value. Venus is why it fails with an atmosphere: mean 464 °C against a distance that predicts far less (NSSDCA overview table, https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/).

## 3. Surviving it

### Methods (all real)

- **Electric heaters on batteries.** Works but is heavy: Ulamec et al. 2010 compute that a lander needing an average of 15 W through a night would store "about 5 kWh", "a battery mass of about 37 kg" with 170 Wh/kg Li-ion. Apollo ALSEP's central station used controlled electric heaters at night (RTG-powered).
- **Radioisotope heater units (RHU).** NASA: each RHU "generates about one watt of heat energy" from a small pellet of plutonium dioxide, mostly plutonium-238, with no moving parts, https://science.nasa.gov/resource/radioisotope-heater-units-fact-sheet/ . Apollo 11's passive seismometer used two 15 W Apollo Lunar Radioisotopic Heaters of 36 g Pu-238 each (Ulamec et al. 2010). Lunokhod used polonium-210 (half-life 138 days) heaters, so the heat faded over months.
- **Insulation.** Multi-layer insulation: "a realistic assumption for thermal loss of a lander, well insulated with MLI is h = 0.04 W/K/m²" (Ulamec et al.). Surveyor showed well-insulated electronics "may survive several Lunar nights".
- **Sleep or dormancy.** Lunokhod and Chang'e rovers shut down at night and woke when sunlight returned; Lunokhod closed a lid with its solar panel on the inside.
- **Thermal storage.** Phase-change material (water or ammonium chloride ice) as a heat battery: about 30-35 L of water for a 1 m² package (Ulamec et al.).
- **Heat in the ground.** Below about one metre the regolith temperature is nearly constant (Ulamec et al.); sites like lunar pits stay around 17 °C (NASA, above).

### Energy to stay warm through one lunar night

One real, published figure: **about 3700 Wh for one lunar night (384 h) for a 1 m² well-insulated package kept at 20 °C**, a loss of "in the order of 9.6 W"; about 3200 Wh if cooling to -40 °C is allowed. ESA/DLR, Ulamec et al. 2010 (above). The authors call it a "very rough back of the envelope calculation" (MLI at 0.04 W/K/m², one half-space at deep space 2.7 K, one at the 100 K surface, no conduction to the ground). Storing 3700 Wh in Li-ion would take "in the order of 20 kg" of battery system, or about 37 kg for 5 kWh. For comparison, an RHU gives about 1 W of heat continuously (NASA): 384 h of one RHU is about 384 Wh of heat, so roughly ten RHUs would cover the 9.6 W loss (our arithmetic).

## 5. Gameplay simplifications (not sourced facts)

These are suggestions for post-v1 design, separate from the facts above.

- Compress day length: one in-game Day-Night Cycle per Body at a fixed short real-time length, with night about half. Real lengths differ hugely (Ceres 9 hours, Mars 24.7 h, Moon 29.5 days, Mercury about 176 days); a game might show the real figure in the info panel and use a scaled factor.
- A single "heat" band per Body: hot inner Bodies (Mercury: day 590-725 K, night about 93 K from -180 °C) punish noon; cold outer Bodies (Europa about 86-132 K, Ceres under about 240 K) punish constant cold. Equilibrium scaling gives a quick tuning rule: temperature in kelvin falls with the square root of distance (254 K at 1 AU, about 111 K at 5.2 AU, same albedo).
- Tune a lander's night as "stored heat needed" with the one real anchor: roughly 3,700 Wh per 1 m² lander-night at 20 °C on the Moon, or about ten RHUs. Alternative simple rules: an RHU-equipped lander survives the night; a battery lander survives only if its battery holds enough; a lander with neither goes dormant and may or may not wake (SLIM and Surveyor woke, Odysseus did not).
- Make the polar permanently shadowed crater the "ice and cold" hazard (25 K) and the sunlit polar rim (about 220 K) the safe power spot, echoing the Space Apps challenge.
- One sentence per Body is enough; do not model albedo or atmosphere in the first pass.

## 6. Suggested text for the game

- "Night is coming: with no air to hold the heat, the ground cools to about minus 170 °C for two weeks, so keep your lander warm or it will freeze."
- Shorter: "The Sun is setting and the cold is coming. Heaters need power; without them the lander freezes."
